We investigate how irradiation redistributes alloying elements near grain boundaries. By coupling defect generation, transport and absorption to solute evolution, we examine why chromium enriches or depletes at interfaces.

## Dilute Fe–Cr: shifting the enrichment-to-depletion transition

We collaborated with colleagues at Idaho National Laboratory to model radiation-induced segregation in **Fe–0.1 at.% Cr**. Under symmetric defect production and absorption, temperature-dependent transport controls the transition from chromium enrichment to depletion. Preferential effective vacancy production or preferential interstitial absorption shifts that transition toward lower temperatures.

[Read the Fe–Cr study: Oplinger et al., *Journal of Applied Physics* 139, 155903 (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0323296).

## Ni–Cr: how production bias changes chromium depletion

In our earlier Ni–Cr study, we examined how an effective imbalance between vacancy and interstitial production changes segregation. Positive vacancy production bias increases chromium depletion in the modeled conditions. We investigated the effects of grain size, dose rate, temperature, sink density and grain-boundary absorption, and compared predictions with published coherent- and incoherent-twin-boundary experiments.

[Read the Ni–Cr study: Gencturk, Ozturk and Ahmed, *Materials* 16(23), 7475 (2023)](https://doi.org/10.3390/ma16237475).

## Connecting the studies

Across these studies, we examine a common mechanism: unequal vacancy and interstitial fluxes change solute redistribution. We consider alloy transport properties together with defect sources and sinks, while keeping the material parameters and segregation predictions for Fe–Cr and Ni–Cr distinct.

These rate-theory studies explain mechanisms within the investigated alloys and irradiation conditions. Our comparisons use published experimental evidence; they do not constitute new irradiation experiments or universal predictions for structural steels.
