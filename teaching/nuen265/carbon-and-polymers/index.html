1 of 7 · Carbon & diamond

How can one element make such different structures?

Diamond forms a three-dimensional network of tetrahedral sp³ bonds. Graphene has planar sp² bonding with three nearest carbon neighbors. Bonding and connectivity change even though the chemical element stays carbon.

Why does diamond persist at ambient conditions?

Graphite is thermodynamically favored at ordinary pressure and room temperature, but diamond persists because changing its bond network requires crossing a large kinetic barrier. Diamond is stable in an appropriate high-pressure region; this sketch describes ambient conditions only.

Bonding → geometry → solid

What do sp² and sp³ tell us?

Hybridization is a localized orbital model for bonding and geometry, not a literal sequence of atomic orbitals mixing over time. Choose a model below; the main structure view above follows your choice.

Orbital/overlap schematic only. Lobes are not atoms or calculated electron-density contours.

σ (sigma) bonding involves overlap along the internuclear direction; π (pi) bonding arises from sideways overlap of suitable p orbitals. In graphene, the π system extends over the sheet. It does not create strong covalent bridges between graphite layers.

Graphite’s easy layer sliding reflects weak interlayer cohesion relative to its in-plane covalent bonding. Electrical transport involves its electronic states, including the delocalized π system, and is much easier along layers. Heat transport is also strongly directional. In diamond, the rigid three-dimensional bond network carries heat effectively through lattice vibrations even though undoped diamond has few mobile electrical carriers. High thermal conductivity does not require high electrical conductivity.

Bonding supports these qualitative connections; this drawing does not calculate a band structure, stiffness, conductivity or a thermal transport coefficient.

2 of 7 · Graphene & graphite

Does AB stacking make graphite HCP?

Graphene is a honeycomb of carbon atoms. Its translation lattice is triangular (2D hexagonal), with a two-carbon basis. Graphite repeats honeycomb layers in an AB sequence; its atomic layers are not close-packed triangular layers.

Graphene & graphite

Read the sheet before stacking it

The outlined rhombus is a primitive sheet cell. The highlighted pair is the two-carbon basis. Here the two equal translation vectors are 60° apart; the equivalent 120° crystallographic convention describes the same lattice.

a=3dCC

Using the lecture’s rounded bond length 0.142 nm gives a lattice length of 0.246 nm. AB shifts the second sheet by one C–C bond vector. A third sheet returns to A. The graphite repeat spans two layers and contains four atoms.

At rest the teaching layer spacing is 0.335 nm. Separating layers is a viewing aid; no energy or property is calculated. In-plane bonding is strong; interlayer interactions are much weaker. Thermal and electrical transport therefore depend strongly on direction.

Nuclear connection: graphite serves as a neutron moderator in graphite-moderated reactors. Its crystal structure helps explain directional behavior, but this model does not calculate neutron slowing or bulk engineering properties.

Keep chemistry in the basis

Graphene has two carbon atoms per primitive cell. Monolayer h-BN has one B and one N on the two honeycomb sublattices. A valid translation must reproduce both positions and chemical identities.

The specified MoS₂ monolayer is 1H, trigonal-prismatic, corresponding to one sandwich of the common bulk 2H structure. It is not the octahedral 1T form. Each interior Mo has three S neighbors above and three below. The upper and lower S positions align in top view.

One in-plane primitive cell contains one Mo and two S. The outlined cell marks only the in-plane repetition; it is not an isolated MoS₂ molecule.

Ideal graphene is a semimetal, h-BN an insulator and this MoS₂ monolayer a semiconductor. These differences require electronic evidence; similar hexagonal patterns alone do not establish electronic behavior.

5 of 7 · Networks & glasses

Can local order survive without a repeating unit cell?

Silica can retain tetrahedral local coordination while the larger arrangement changes. A glass lacks the long-range translational repetition of a crystal; a small local motif alone cannot establish crystallinity.

Networks & glasses

The tetrahedral view is a local coordination motif. The other two views are labeled connectivity schematics; neither claims to be a crystallographic SiO₂ unit cell.

Drag or use arrow keys to rotate. Top/Front show orthographic projections.

Local coordination is not long-range order

The local view shows four oxygen neighbors around silicon. In the ideal fully connected silica network, each oxygen bridges two silicon centers. Sharing makes the overall formula SiO₂ even though the local unit is called SiO₄.

The network views use ◇ symbols for tetrahedral building blocks and lines for connections. They are deliberately 2D connectivity cartoons, not coordinates of quartz or an atomistic glass. Ring shapes and sizes are illustrative, not measured statistics.

The ordered cartoon repeats a motif; the glass cartoon interrupts that repetition. An actual amorphous network must be characterized over larger distances. Merely displacing a few atoms or drawing a short irregular fragment does not prove that a bulk material is glass.

Cooling can arrest structural rearrangement before crystallization occurs. The following polymer tabs use the same distinction between local chain structure and the arrangement of many chains.

Choose the counting convention first

Here n counts ethylene-derived two-carbon units in PE. The smallest constitutional repeating unit is –CH₂–, so the same chain contains 2n of those minimal units. The gold outline highlights the first two-carbon repeat.

For the mass exercise only, specify one extra terminal H at each end: H–(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–H. The resulting formula is C₂ₙH₄ₙ₊₂.

Mchain=n×28.054+2.016

The displayed molar mass is in g/mol. Rounded atomic molar masses: C = 12.011 and H = 1.008 g/mol. This is a single H-capped oligomer model, not a measured polymer molar-mass distribution or a polymerization mechanism. At n = 1 it is ethane, not a high polymer.

Switching to PP, PVC or PTFE compares substituents and repeat chemistry. The PE-specific mass calculation is then hidden. Side groups alter interactions and packing; they do not supply numerical properties by themselves.

PE chains interact mainly through dispersion forces; they are not universally hydrogen bonded. Other polymer chemistries can hydrogen bond, and some have non-carbon backbones.

Inside a chain / between chains

Which connections survive when chains move?

Strong covalent bonds form each backbone; a covalent crosslink joins different chains. Secondary interactions also help hold chains together, but they can rearrange during molecular motion without cutting the backbone.

Solid: covalent backbone or crosslink. Dots: dispersion. Dashes: selected polar/H-bond interaction. Separation is an arbitrary drawing displacement, not a force or energy calculation.

Dispersion acts between all chains through fluctuating charge distributions. Permanent dipoles add orientation-dependent interactions when the chemistry is polar. Hydrogen bonding needs suitable donor/acceptor groups: for example, N–H donors and carbonyl O acceptors in polyamides. PE has neither these N–H/O–H donors nor carbonyl acceptors; its H atoms do not make PE a hydrogen-bonded polymer.

Hydrogen bonds are not covalent crosslinks. A covalent network constrains long-range chain sliding even when some secondary contacts rearrange. The optional crosslink illustrates that distinction without specifying a real crosslinking reaction.

Chain architecture and chain packing describe different features. A linear chain need not be geometrically straight, and a polymer can contain both ordered and amorphous regions.

Architecture and thermal response

Linear: no branch junctions. Branched: side chains attach to a backbone. Crosslinked: covalent connections join chains into a network. Maroon transverse links remain joined when the sketch is stretched.

Thermoplastics can soften and flow when sufficiently heated, if degradation does not intervene. Elastomeric behavior often involves lightly crosslinked flexible chains above their glass transition. Thermosets form permanent networks that inhibit long-range sliding and do not freely melt-flow.

Tg marks a glass-transition range associated with segmental mobility in amorphous material. Tm concerns loss of crystalline order. A fully amorphous polymer has no crystalline melting transition; a semicrystalline polymer can exhibit both Tg and melting.

The aligned central portions of the semicrystalline sketch represent ordered regions. Selecting the final heating stage removes their alignment. This is a teaching animation, not a crystal structure, crystallinity measurement or molecular-dynamics result.

Thermal degradation changes chemical structure and is distinct from softening or melting. There is no universal 200 °C limit and no fixed Tg-to-Tm ratio here. Material identity, time and environment matter.

Order at two length scales

Polymer crystallinity uses the same definition of order

A crystalline region has regular three-dimensional packing. In a polymer, portions of long chains can pack into small ordered regions, often thin lamellae, while other portions remain disordered. A tie chain connects ordered regions through the intervening amorphous material. A chain may therefore contribute segments to both environments.

This is not a replacement for the usual crystal definition. Metals and ceramics can also be polycrystalline, glassy or partly crystalline. Merely repeating a monomer along one chain—or aligning chains without positional registry—does not establish a crystal.

Lamella/tie-chain schematic, not atomic coordinates. Shaded regions are not a measurement of crystalline area or volume. The numerical example uses a supplied mass partition.

Xc,m=mcmc+ma

mc: crystalline mass; ma: amorphous mass. Degree of crystallinity is a fraction, not the fraction of chains that are entirely crystalline. Experimental estimates from diffraction or calorimetry need defined methods and reference states; mass and volume fractions are not interchangeable.

Motion / order / chemical identity

Crossing Tg does not cut the polymer chain

Tg is a glass-transition range associated with cooperative segmental rearrangements in amorphous material becoming accessible on the observation timescale. Below Tg, vibrations and some local motions still occur; large-scale segmental rearrangements are slow. Above Tg, more conformations can be explored, stiffness usually decreases and time-dependent deformation becomes more pronounced. The observed Tg depends on timescale, thermal history and chemistry.

Tm is different: crystalline regions lose their ordered packing on melting. Between Tg and Tm a semicrystalline polymer has more mobile amorphous regions while crystallites still constrain it. An amorphous thermoplastic can soften and eventually flow with sufficient heating/time without a crystalline melting transition. Permanent crosslinks constrain flow even above Tg. Many elastomers operate above Tg; thermoplastic elastomers use other reversible constraints and are not all permanently crosslinked.

Discrete motion frames illustrate mobility, not dynamics. Cooling can allow crystallization where chemistry and time permit; the restored ordered sketch is a possible state, not a predicted cooling rate or instantaneous process.

Use the distinctions to reason about processing

Melting, degradation and recyclability

Melting removes crystalline order while the polymer molecules can remain chemically intact. Degradation changes chemistry: chain scission, oxidation or changes in crosslinking can damage performance. It is not synonymous with melting or always with complete depolymerization back to monomers. It depends on temperature, time, oxygen, moisture, light and the particular polymer.

One chain fragment is traced here. A line gap means a broken covalent bond; it is not a melted contact. Other degradation pathways are not drawn.

Thermoplastics are candidates for reshaping, but collection, sorting, contamination, additives, degradation, process design and economics determine practical recycling. Repeated heating and shear can change molecular mass or chemistry; there is no universal safe number of cycles.

Conventional crosslinked thermosets do not simply remelt into an equivalent moldable material. That does not exclude every recovery route: grinding for filler, recovery of reinforcing fibers or chemistry-specific bond-cleavage/reprocessing methods may be useful. Designed recyclable networks are special cases, not evidence that every thermoset can be remelted.
