From crystal symmetry to material density

Start with a lattice and its atomic basis. Test symmetry, count atoms, identify neighbors, and connect crystal geometry to material density.

Start here · Why count lattices?

Bravais lattices classify repeating point patterns

In the nineteenth century, crystallographers asked how many fundamentally different periodic point arrangements can fill three-dimensional space. The difficulty was to count distinct lattice types, rather than every cell shape used to draw them. Early classifications were not all correct: Frankenheim reported 15 types in 1842, one more than the accepted total.

Auguste Bravais established the correct classification of 14 three-dimensional lattice types in 1850. A Bravais lattice has equivalent points: a translation from any one point to any other reproduces the whole array. Different cell sizes and alternative cell choices can describe the same type. These fourteen types classify the repeating framework; they do not mean that only fourteen crystals or materials can exist. Historical account in the Journal of Applied Crystallography.

A lattice is the repeating framework; the basis supplies the atoms

A lattice point is an abstract position. A basis is an atom or group of atoms attached in the same orientation to every lattice point. Repeating that basis builds a crystal structure.

Lattice + atomic basis = crystal structure

1D · One Bravais lattice type

On a line, equally spaced equivalent points form the only Bravais lattice type. Choose a spacing a; every point has position x = na, with integer n. Changing a stretches the lattice without creating a new type.

Adding identical midpoint sites simply gives a smaller spacing a/2. Alternating different atoms changes the basis, not the number of 1D Bravais types.

2D · Five Bravais lattice types

Two independent translations tile a plane. Edge lengths, their angle, and the equivalence of all points leave five distinct types. The shaded outline is a cell; dots continue beyond its boundaries.

The centered rectangle contains two lattice points per conventional cell; a smaller primitive rhombus contains one. The 2D hexagonal lattice is a triangular point net, not a honeycomb: a honeycomb needs a two-point basis. Special dimensions can raise symmetry, so the sketches use representative non-degenerate examples.

One material can have different crystal structures

Polymorphism is the ability of a solid of the same chemical composition to exist in more than one crystal structure. For an element, this is called allotropy. These are general features of materials: carbon has diamond and graphite, iron has BCC and FCC phases, and compounds can also have multiple crystal forms.

Temperature and pressure can change which structure is stable. Compare uranium, plutonium and thorium, three elements relevant to nuclear fuels. Select a metal, then one of its illustrated phases. 02 · Symmetry

Does the entire pattern match after the operation?

A symmetry operation moves a pattern so that it is indistinguishable from the original. A translation shifts every point by the same vector; a rotation turns every point around an axis. The test concerns the complete repeating pattern, including its basis—not just the outline of one cell.

Drag a 3D diagram to rotate, or focus it and use arrow keys. Spheres are reduced in size to reveal the layer positions.

What makes a Bravais lattice?

For a Bravais point lattice, a translation from any lattice point to any other must reproduce the entire array. A crystal with several atoms in its basis does not need a translation connecting every pair of atoms. Other symmetry operations can relate those atoms.

“Not a Bravais lattice” does not mean “an impossible crystal.” It means the complete atomic pattern needs a basis on an underlying Bravais lattice, or that a proposed cell can be redrawn as a type already counted.

03 · From layers to a three-dimensional crystal

Same close-packed layer, different stacking

Begin with identical touching spheres in a triangular layer. Each sphere touches six neighbors in that plane. The letters A, B and C name lateral positions of whole layers—not different elements, atom types or heights.

Call the first layer A. Its triangular hollows form two alternating sets, which we label B and C. A second close-packed layer can occupy one set of hollows; filling both sets at the same height would overlap the spheres.

1 · Find the three layer positions

Top view: circles in different layers overlap in projection, not in three-dimensional space. Hollow-site symbols mark potential sphere centers; they are not extra atoms.

Geometry of the A, B and C positions

Take in-plane translation vectors a₁ = a(1, 0) and a₂ = a(½, √3/2), where a = 2R is the nearest-neighbor distance within a layer.

A: 0
B: (a₁ + a₂)/3
C: 2(a₁ + a₂)/3

Positions repeat modulo a₁ and a₂. Both sets of hollows are a/√3 from three surrounding A atoms. The ideal vertical layer spacing is h = a√(2/3), so the three-dimensional contact distance is √[(a/√3)² + h²] = a = 2R.

Here a is the in-plane triangular spacing. It is the HCP basal lattice length; for FCC it equals the cubic cell edge divided by √2.

2 · Choose the next layer

Never repeat the immediately preceding letter when building a close-packed equal-sphere stack: no AA, BB or CC. A letter may reappear after another layer—ABA is allowed.

Up to 12 layers. This builds a finite sequence; a repeating crystal is obtained by continuing a pattern. Other allowed sequences and stacking faults are possible. Geometry alone does not decide which is energetically stable.

What changes when the third layer is chosen?

HCP · ABAB…

The third layer lies directly above the first. The two-layer repeat gives hexagonal close-packed stacking, with layer normal along the hexagonal c axis.

FCC · ABCABC…

The third layer occupies the remaining registry. The three-layer repeat gives cubic close packing: these layers are the FCC {111} planes. ACBACB… gives the equivalent reverse stacking sense.

Both ideal structures have 12 nearest neighbors (6 in the same layer, 3 above and 3 below) and a 74.0% packing fraction. Equal coordination and packing do not mean identical translational symmetry.

AA stacking can exist in a less densely packed structure. Directly aligned spheres require a vertical separation of at least 2R, greater than the close-packed spacing h. The “no repeat” rule applies to close packing, not to all crystals.

Why focus on cubic cells and HCP?

These simple structures cover most of the elemental solids in our defined room-temperature census: 54 of 83 elements (about 65%) adopt SC, BCC, FCC or HCP. That makes them a useful starting point for counting atoms, coordination and density.

“Cubic” or “hexagonal” alone does not imply a simple atomic structure. Diamond, graphite, α-Mn and double-HCP metals require different bases or stacking; they are not counted as these four simple structures.

07 · Theoretical density

Mass per cell ÷ cell volume

A unit cell is a representative repeating piece of a perfect crystal. Repeating it multiplies mass and volume equally, so its density is the density of the ideal material.

We call this the theoretical density: real samples may contain porosity, vacancies, composition variations or secondary phases that change the measured bulk density. Compare values at the same temperature, because the lattice dimensions also change with temperature.

g/cm³
ρ = nM / (NAVcell)

n = atoms per cell; M = molar mass; Vcell = cell volume; NA = 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. Use consistent mass and length units.

Fourteen lattice types; far more crystal structures

Seven crystal systems and fourteen Bravais lattice types classify periodic symmetry and translation patterns in three dimensions. They do not limit the number of crystal structures to fourteen. Changing the basis, composition and atomic positions gives an unbounded number of mathematically possible structures; which are stable materials is a separate physical question.

From touching atoms to cell volume

1 · Cubic cells: follow the touching direction

The edge length a is not always one atomic diameter. Trace a row of touching atoms first, equate its geometrical length to the sum of atomic radii, and solve for a/R. These are the closest-packed directions in these hard-sphere models; BCC is not a close-packed structure like FCC or HCP.

2 · Hexagonal cells: basal area × height

A primitive basal parallelogram has area a² sin120° = (√3/2)a². The drawing uses the equivalent 60° basal-vector choice, which gives the same area. The large hexagon contains six equilateral triangles, each of area √3a²/4. Multiply either area by the cell height c:

Vprimitive = (√3/2)a²c
Vlarge prism = (3√3/2)a²c = 3Vprimitive

For HCP these cells contain 2 and 6 atoms, respectively. The hexagonal Bravais lattice itself has a one-point primitive cell.

h² + (a/√3)² = a² → h = a√(2/3)
c = 2h → (c/a)ideal = √(8/3) ≈ 1.633

Here h is the A-to-B layer spacing; the next A layer is at c = 2h. This ratio assumes identical touching spheres. Real HCP metals can depart from it: the density presets give Mg ≈1.6235 and Zr ≈1.5925. With nonideal c/a, the basal and out-of-plane neighbor distances differ.

3 · A formula for any crystal system

For the parallelepiped defined by vectors a, b, c, its positive volume is the magnitude of their scalar triple product:

V = |a · (b × c)|
V = abc √[1 + 2 cosα cosβ cosγ − cos²α − cos²β − cos²γ]

α is between b and c; β between a and c; γ between a and b. Lengths use the same units; the angle combination must describe a nonzero three-dimensional cell. For a large hexagonal prism, calculate its primitive parallelepiped volume and multiply by three.

The simple-cubic exception

Why does α-polonium choose simple cubic?

α-Po is the only element with a monatomic simple-cubic crystal structure under ambient conditions. The qualification matters: other elements can become simple cubic at high pressure, and polonium has other phases. Legut’s clarification; X-ray identification of α-Po.

Polonium is radioactive and has no stable isotopes. For example, ²¹⁰Po emits alpha particles and has a half-life of about 138.4 days. The α in “α-Po” names a crystal phase; it is not a label for its radiation. Radioactive decay concerns the nucleus, whereas the preference for the SC lattice is explained through electronic bonding. Royal Society of Chemistry: polonium.

Why is SC rare among metals?

SC gives each atom only 6 nearest neighbors and fills 52.4% of the cell in the touching-sphere model. FCC and ideal HCP have 12 neighbors and fill 74.0%. Many metals gain more bonding energy in arrangements with more neighbors.

But packing is a clue, not the final rule: the stable structure minimizes free energy. Electronic bonding, repulsion and temperature all matter. A metal need not choose the densest geometrical packing.

Why is Po different?

Po is a heavy element (Z = 84). Relativistic effects change its electron energies and bonding, stabilizing the SC arrangement. These effects reduce the energetic advantage of the lower-symmetry, unequal-bond arrangements favored by its lighter relatives Se and Te.

A useful picture is that the electrons can sometimes lower their energy by shifting atoms and making some bonds shorter and others longer—a Peierls-type distortion. In Po, relativistic changes help the undistorted cubic arrangement remain favorable. Legut et al. (2007); Min et al. (2006).

Count it: 8 corners × ⅛ = 1 atom per SC cell. Its low packing fraction does not prevent α-Po from being the energetically preferred structure.
One step deeper: which relativistic effect?

Calculations agree that relativity is important, but differ in the emphasis. Legut and co-workers identify scalar-relativistic changes (mass–velocity and Darwin terms); Min and co-workers emphasize spin–orbit coupling, the interaction between electron spin and orbital motion, suppressing a Peierls instability. The introductory takeaway is that electronic energy determines the structure, not packing alone.

Plutonium: an unusually rich set of allotropes

Pure Pu has six solid allotropes at ambient pressure, the largest count commonly cited for a metal under that condition. In order of increasing temperature, they are α, β, γ, δ, δ′ and ε. A seventh form, ζ, has been reported under pressure; it is not another member of this ambient-pressure sequence. Martz et al.; LANL Pu overview.

Why a large basis can be favorable

  • Electronic bonding: different bond lengths and directions can lower the electronic energy; atoms need not behave like equally packed hard spheres.
  • Several local environments: one repeating motif can accommodate inequivalent atomic sites. β-U has five distinct site groups.
  • Free-energy competition: temperature-dependent electronic and vibrational contributions can change which arrangement is stable.

What the large number does not mean

It does not create a new Bravais type. It also does not prove that one cell is physically larger than another.

Drawing a supercell multiplies the displayed atom count without changing the crystal. In contrast, β-U’s thirty-atom primitive basis cannot be reduced simply by choosing a smaller translation cell.

In compounds and ordered alloys, arranging different chemical species can also enlarge the repeating motif. β-U and Mn are elemental examples, so chemical ordering is not their explanation.

For β-U’s inequivalent electronic environments see Qiu et al., β-U electronic structure. The Pu bonding discussion above provides a related actinide example; these are physical reasons for structural complexity, not a calculation of which phase is stable.

8 × ⅛ + 2 × ½ = 2 lattice points

Why the symmetry matters

Orthorhombic α-U can respond differently along its three crystal axes; its strong directional thermal expansion and irradiation growth matter in metallic fuels. Cubic γ-U has higher symmetry, although cubic symmetry does not make every property isotropic.

Assumptions, notation & sources

Atoms are modeled as identical spheres for counting and ideal packing. The sphere-size slider changes the drawing only; it does not change atomic packing fraction or density. In the unit-cell and coordination tabs, HCP uses ideal c/a = √(8/3) ≈ 1.633. Actual HCP metals may differ: the Zr preset has c/a ≈ 1.5925, so its six basal and six out-of-plane near neighbors are at slightly different distances.

Density assumes a fully occupied, pure, single-phase crystal. The experimental lattice dimensions already describe its volume at the stated temperature; no hard-sphere radius assumption is needed for this calculation. Editing a preset creates a custom example and does not predict phase stability, thermal expansion, or an alloy density.
