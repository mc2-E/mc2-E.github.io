From ion sizes to crystal structures

In elemental metals, we often pack equal-sized atoms. In a compound, different species must fit together. Start with their relative sizes to estimate local coordination; then see how repeating sets of sites combine into the complete crystal.

  1. Compare sizes
  2. Estimate coordination
  3. Check real crystals
  4. Combine sublattices

1 · Start with the cation-to-anion size ratio

A cation is positively charged; an anion is negatively charged. In many familiar ionic compounds, the cation is smaller. The relative size helps us reason about how many anions can surround it, rather than comparing the two radii separately.

q=r+r−

Here r₊ is the cation radius and r₋ is the anion radius; q has no units. A small q means a relatively small cation. As q approaches one, the radii become more similar.

Try NaCl first, then ZnS and CsCl. Changing a radius is a hypothetical size comparison; it does not change the material preset.

2 · Use the ratio to estimate local coordination

Coordination number (CN) counts an ion’s nearest neighbors of the opposite species. In the ideal hard-sphere picture, a larger cation relative to the anion can accommodate more surrounding anions. The radius-ratio rule gives the following first estimate of the cation’s coordination.

Ideal radius-ratio guide · local neighbors around one cation
Ratio qCation CNArrangement of neighboring anions

0.155 to below 0.2253Triangular
0.225 to below 0.4144Tetrahedral
0.414 to below 0.7326Octahedral
0.732 to below 18Cubic

Thresholds are rounded here; the estimate uses the exact geometric limits. This simple guide assumes the cation is smaller than the anion. At equal or larger cation size, it does not assign a coordination here.

Where do the thresholds come from?

At each limiting geometry, the central cation touches every surrounding anion while neighboring anions also touch. Geometry gives the minimum radius ratio needed to support that ideal contact arrangement. For an octahedral cage, the limit is:

qmin, CN6=2−1≈0.414

Tab 2 develops the related geometry of interstitial sites. There, the question is the largest guest that fits an unchanged host; here, the contact geometry is used as a rough guide to ionic coordination.

Local geometry comes first. Six surrounding anions define an octahedron around one cation. They do not, by themselves, specify the whole unit cell. The way these local environments repeat and share ions determines the full structure.

Geometry: MIT, Radius Ratios and Symmetry in Ionic Crystals, Table 1; compare NUEN265 Lecture 6, slides 9–11.

3 · Compare the estimate with real crystal structures

ZnS, NaCl and CsCl illustrate four-, six- and eightfold coordination. UO₂ shows why a size rule is only a starting point: its supplied ratio is about 0.725, yet each U has eight O neighbors in fluorite. Bonding, charge and the energetics of the complete structure also matter.

Each sketch shows a complete conventional cell with reduced spheres, not a local coordination cage or radii drawn to scale. Select “Explore” to load its radii above.

How the reference radii were selected

Radius convention: all values use Shannon’s effective ionic radii (IR column). Radius CN is the coordination used in the source table; crystal CN describes the actual structure. For ZnS and CsCl, the available anion radius is a CN 6 reference, identified on the card. Their crystal coordination is unchanged.

Radii already depend on oxidation state and coordination, so this exercise helps explain structures rather than independently predicting them from first principles. Formal ion labels are bookkeeping, not maps of measured charge density.

Values: Shannon (1976), Table 1, pp. 752–753.

Scope: the editable ratio gives an ideal coordination estimate; it does not predict a phase change or replace the selected crystal. See Hawthorne & Gagné (2024), section 7.1 for the limitations of radius-ratio predictions.

4 · Extend local packing into a complete crystal

A sublattice is a repeating set of sites within the crystal. Follow one ion species at a time to see its arrangement, then combine the sets to recover the full structure. The size ratio helps us reason about local packing; site occupation, charge balance and the repeating arrangement are also needed to describe the crystal.

NaCl as the worked example: the ratio suggests sixfold coordination, as observed around Na. Na and Cl form two displaced FCC sublattices; each ion has six nearest neighbors of the other species. In the cell drawn below, Na occupies corners and faces, while Cl occupies edge centers and the body center. Together, these positions give one NaCl crystal.

Lattice, basis, and sublattice

Lattice: equivalent mathematical repeat points. Basis: the atom or group of atoms attached to each lattice point. Repeating that basis gives the crystal structure.

A sublattice follows a chosen set of sites through the crystal. The Bravais lattice describes translations that preserve the complete chemical structure. The two are not interchangeable.

Charge neutrality fixes the species ratio

Na⁺ and Cl⁻ balance in a 1:1 ratio; U⁴⁺ needs two O²⁻ ions, giving UO₂. The formula gives that species ratio; a unit cell may contain several formula units. Size alone does not fix how many sites are occupied.

Why these sublattices are not separate phases

A phase is a part of a material system with uniform chemical composition and physical state. For a crystalline solid, identify its structure too. The interpenetrating Na and Cl sublattices together belong to one NaCl phase. Detailed phases and microstructure belong to our later session.

Bonding beyond the ionic picture

Real bonding may mix ionic, covalent and metallic contributions. A shared structure type does not mean identical bonding. UC and UN have the rock-salt geometry without being ordinary NaCl-like salts.

FCC octahedral sites: one at the center plus twelve quarter-owned edge sites. BCC octahedral sites: six half-owned face sites plus twelve quarter-owned edge sites. BCC tetrahedral sites: four per face, each half-owned. Boundary markers show periodic copies of equivalent sites; count their ownership fractions rather than counting symbols. BCC needs its own geometry. Its octahedral cage has two closer and four farther hosts. “Octahedral” describes the six-vertex arrangement, not six equidistant nearest neighbors. The lower APF of BCC does not imply that every individual hole is larger.

Material connections: FCC Ni and austenitic steels; BCC Fe and W; HCP α-Zr. The HCP drawing uses an ideal axial ratio for a geometry comparison. The two-atom rhombic prism is a primitive repeat cell; the familiar six-sided HCP prism contains three such repeat volumes and six atoms.

Derive the ideal guest-size limits

Let R be host radius and r the largest guest radius at the selected site, without moving the hosts. Subtract R from the shortest site-to-host distance.

rR=32−1≈0.225

FCC/ideal HCP tetrahedral: for FCC, the (¼,¼,¼) site is a34 from a host; R=a24. Octahedral: the nearest distance is a2, giving rR=2−1≈0.414.

BCC: R=a34. At an octahedral edge-center site the closest distance is a2, giving rR=23−1≈0.155. At (0,¼,½), the tetrahedral distance is a54, giving rR=53−1≈0.291.

These are geometric limits. Relaxation and bonding determine real occupation and solubility.

Inspect a complete fluorite cell

Start with the complete cell. The optional Build this cell step by step button separates framework corners, framework faces, empty sites, and their occupation.

Count, then simplify. The conventional fluorite cell contains 4 framework atoms and 8 tetrahedral-site atoms. Its 12 atoms are four AB₂ formula units—not twelve formula units.

The eight internal sites form a small cube, with each fractional coordinate equal to ¼ or ¾. Use the complete-cell overview to see the outer FCC framework and the inner cube together. Then choose a species to open a separate neighbor-cage drawing; it is not another unit cell.

Nuclear examples with a common structure

UO₂, ThO₂ and PuO₂ share the ideal fluorite arrangement. Uranium/plutonium oxides connect to nuclear fuels and mixed-oxide fuels; ThO₂ connects to thorium-bearing fuels, where Th-232 is fertile. These application labels do not change the geometrical counting.

In the ideal formal-charge picture, one tetravalent metal ion balances two O²⁻ ions. CaF₂ instead pairs Ca²⁺ with two F⁻ ions. For antifluorite Li₂O, O occupies the FCC framework and Li fills all tetrahedral sites.

Why the coordination numbers differ

Each framework atom has eight neighbors on the occupied tetrahedral sites; each tetrahedral-site atom has four framework neighbors. Counting the same A–B links from both ends gives 4×8 = 8×4 = 32 links per conventional cell.

This is a construction of ideal positions, not an oxidation animation or a defect-equilibrium model. Periodic neighbors appear only in the separately selected coordination close-up.

Two useful ways to count

Corundum: start with an approximately HCP oxygen framework. There is one candidate octahedral site per oxygen atom; occupy two-thirds with Al. For 18 O, 12 Al occupy the appropriate sites, giving Al₂O₃. The actual hexagonal-setting cell displayed here contains 30 atoms and six formula units. It is not the simple six-atom HCP prism.

Ideal cubic perovskite: A on corners, B at the body center and X on face centers. Boundary ownership gives 1 A, 1 B and 3 X. Each B is surrounded by six X atoms; neighboring BX₆ octahedra share corners.

An unoccupied member of a possible site family can be part of an ideal crystal structure. It should not automatically be called a vacancy defect.
Charge bookkeeping and structural qualifications

Al³⁺ and O²⁻ give 2(+3)+3(−2)=0. For an oxide perovskite with A²⁺ and three O²⁻, B must be +4. The BaTiO₃ illustration is the ideal cubic, high-temperature structure; ambient BaTiO₃ is not represented by this cubic preset.

Corundum uses a rhombohedral translation lattice displayed in its larger hexagonal setting. Its six Al–O neighbors need not all lie at exactly the same distance. The rounded internal coordinates are for teaching geometry; no density calculation uses this preset.

Alloys: chemical order and positional order

A disordered solid solution is still a crystal. “Disordered” can describe which species occupies each lattice site without removing the repeating arrangement of the sites themselves.

Two distinct meanings of disorder. Chemical disorder concerns the identities on available sites. Positional (physical) disorder concerns where atoms sit. A substitutionally disordered crystal retains long-range order in its average atomic positions. Thermal vibrations and isolated defects do not by themselves make it amorphous.

Read the three sketches from left to right. Each uses the same 20 positions; the last two also contain the same 10 A and 10 B. Only the occupation pattern changes. These are 2D teaching diagrams, not new crystal types. Chemical disorder can retain short-range preferences.

Parent geometry versus chemical translation symmetry

Ordered B2 NiAl has two distinct corner/body-center sublattices. Ordered L1₂ Ni₃Al has Al on corners and Ni on faces. Their chemical translation lattices are primitive cubic even though their parent position geometries are BCC and FCC.

Randomly assigning chemical species to those parent sites removes the small repeating chemical pattern. The average host positions still describe a crystal. A particular finite random arrangement is not itself a perfectly repeated small chemical basis.

Composition and nuclear-material connections

The Fe–Ni–Cr example contains 22 Fe, 4 Ni and 6 Cr atoms in a 32-site FCC array. This is an illustrative composition, not a full 316 microstructure or phase-stability model. Changing carbon occupation illustrates counting, not solubility. Interstitial carbon may strain its host; this rigid-position drawing does not calculate relaxation.

In a specified homogeneous mixed oxide (U,Pu)O₂, U and Pu can share the cation sublattice while the ideal oxygen framework remains. A ceramic can therefore also contain chemical substitutional disorder without losing its crystal structure.

6.3 · Reasoning / calculation

A 2 × 2 × 2 L1₂ supercell contains 8 Al and 24 Ni atoms. Swap two Al atoms with two Ni atoms. What changes, and what remains unchanged?

Hint

Each exchange transfers an identity between sublattices without adding or moving a site.

Reveal explanation
The totals remain 8 Al and 24 Ni, or 25 atomic % Al. Sublattice occupation and chemical order change, but the site coordinates and overall composition are unchanged. The structure can remain crystalline.
6.4 · Reasoning / calculation

Suppose a chemically random alloy is drawn on exact FCC positions. A second drawing moves atoms slightly about those sites. Must the second drawing be amorphous?

Hint

Distinguish a displacement from loss of long-range average positional order.

Reveal explanation
No. Crystals have thermal displacements and can contain defects. Small departures from ideal positions do not prove amorphous structure. The relevant distinction is whether long-range order in the average positions persists.
