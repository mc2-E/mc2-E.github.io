Direction-labeling recipe

  1. Locate both endpoints. Read the tail and head using the same origin.
  2. Subtract tail from head. Keep each component’s sign.
  3. Normalize by the cell edges. Divide physical displacements by a, b, c once. The inputs here are already fractional cell coordinates.
  4. Clear fractions and reduce. Multiply every component by the same factor; remove their common integer divisor.
  5. Use square brackets. Write [uvw] without commas, with a bar over each negative index.

Cubic structureDensest direction family
Simple cubic⟨100⟩ · edges

In the identical hard-sphere models, nearest neighbors touch along these rows. The drawn spheres are reduced in size to keep their centers visible.

Why do permutations belong to one family?

In these cubic structures, symmetry exchanges equivalent axes. Thus [100], [010], and [001] are equivalent edge directions. Sign changes give the opposite edges; ⟨100⟩ contains six directed members. The full ⟨110⟩ family has twelve and ⟨111⟩ has eight.

Use family view to inspect signed permutations. This applies to the full cubic symmetry used here. Hexagonal symmetry treats the basal axes and c differently; the HCP tab shows why four-index notation is useful.

Drag or use arrow keys to rotate. Top and Front are orthographic views. An end-on arrow uses a circled dot toward you or a circled cross away.

Plane-labeling recipe

  1. Check the origin. If the plane passes through it, shift the origin or use a parallel plane with finite intercepts.
  2. Read intercepts in cell-edge units. Use a, b, c once. An axis parallel to the plane has an infinite intercept.
  3. Take reciprocals. Infinity becomes zero; negative intercepts remain negative.
  4. Clear fractional indices together. Multiply every reciprocal by the same factor. Keep track of the plane offset if you want the exact original plane.
  5. Use parentheses. Write (hkl) without commas and use overbars for negative indices.
hq+kr+ls=m

The gold markers identify finite axis intercepts A, B, C. The readout lists their coordinates. A zero index means parallel to that axis; it never means a zero intercept.

Which planes are most densely populated?

StructureDensest plane family
Simple cubic{100} · square net
BCC{110} · centered rectangular net
FCC{111} · triangular close-packed net

Strict close packing: FCC {111} is a triangular layer with six touching neighbors per atom. SC and BCC have densest planes, but neither has a truly close-packed plane. BCC {110} is often called “most closely packed” in introductory descriptions.

Plane families, spacing, and the metric

In the full cubic model, axis permutations and allowed sign changes generate equivalent orientations. Braces name the family: {100} includes (100), (010), (001) and their signed partners. Opposite normals describe parallel orientations; they are not different spacings.

dhkl=ah2+k2+l2

(100) and (200) have the same orientation, but different indexed diffraction spacings. Do not silently reduce reflection indices. Clearing the thirds for intercepts (½, 1, ¾) gives 6q + 3r + 4s = 3, preserving the original plane.

Matching [hkl] is normal to (hkl) for every orientation in a cubic metric. Outside cubic metrics, this shortcut is not generally valid, although special directions can still coincide with a normal. The unequal-edge option demonstrates the limitation.

Why use four axes and four indices for HCP?

The three basal axes a₁, a₂, a₃ are equivalent and 120° apart; c is perpendicular and distinct. Rotate an example through equivalent basal orientations and compare its three-index and four-index labels.

Miller–Bravais · HCP

Black dashed arrows: a₁, a₂, a₃, c. Maroon: selected direction. Teal: selected plane clipped to the hexagonal prism. Rotation acts about c; c is never exchanged with a basal axis.

Drag or use arrow keys to rotate. Top and Front are orthographic views. An end-on arrow uses a circled dot toward you or a circled cross away.

Symmetry connects the two notations

In a cubic crystal, exchanging a, b, and c can produce an equivalent orientation: [100], [010], and [001] belong to one family. In a hexagonal crystal, exchanging a basal axis with c does not generally preserve equivalence.

A three-index system using a₁, a₂, c is valid, but singles out two of the three equivalent basal axes. Adding a₃ makes that basal symmetry visible in the indices. Four-index notation describes three-dimensional space with a redundant basal coordinate; it does not add a fourth dimension.

a1+a2+a3=0

Directions: steps to follow

  1. Express the vector using a₁, a₂, c as [UVW].
  2. Compute u = (2U − V)/3 and v = (2V − U)/3.
  3. Set t = −(u + v) and w = W.
  4. Clear fractions in all four entries together, reduce, and write [uvtw].
u=2U−V3,v=2V−U3

Planes: steps to follow

  1. Read intercepts along a₁, a₂, c in their own axis units.
  2. Take reciprocals and clear fractions to obtain h, k, l.
  3. Insert i = −(h + k), then write (hkil).
  4. Check h + k + i = 0. Do not use the direction conversion for planes.

HCP packing: the closest-packed basal directions are ⟨112¯0⟩ and the close-packed basal planes are {0001}. Permuting the basal entries within the hexagonal symmetry can expose equivalents; arbitrary permutations involving the final c index are invalid.

When do scattered X-rays reinforce each other?

X-ray wavelengths are comparable to atomic plane spacings. Parallel planes introduce a path difference. Change spacing, wavelength, and angle to compare that path difference with a whole number of wavelengths. The detector angle is twice the angle to the planes.

Bragg geometry

Horizontal lines represent planes. Incoming and outgoing arrows make θ with them. Dashed ray: straight-through continuation. The wave comparison below uses two equal amplitudes only; it is not a measured diffraction intensity.

Illustrative default: d = λ = 0.200 nm; n = 1.

Read the geometry before the peak

nλ=2dsinθ

Each of the two extra ray segments contributes one spacing times sine of the angle. Constructive interference requires their combined extra path to equal an integer number of wavelengths.

θ is measured between the incoming beam and the planes. 2θ is the change in beam direction from the straight-through continuation to the diffracted ray.

Bragg’s law gives a geometric condition. The atom basis can still cancel a reflection. Continue to reflection rules to check whether a geometrically possible peak is allowed.
Limits and diffraction order

If nλ exceeds 2d, no real Bragg angle exists. Equality gives the limiting backscattering angle θ = 90°. At θ = 0°, the path difference is zero, so no positive-order Bragg condition is met.

Second order from (100) has the same angle as first order indexed (200). In the next tab, the order is kept at one and incorporated in the full reflection indices.

Selection rules have a structural scope

BCC: the sum of the indices must be even. FCC: all three must be even or all three odd. Zero counts as even. Negative indices follow the same parity rules.

These rules follow from summing the phases of identical atoms at the shown fractional coordinates. The live basis factor is the squared magnitude of that sum, divided by the single-atom scattering factor squared.

The rules shown here apply to these particular identical-atom cubic models. A compound basis can change reflection intensities and additional absences. Do not apply these rules automatically to CsCl, diamond, or HCP.

When does a material respond differently in different directions?

Switch between a single crystal and two polycrystals. Watch how the crystal orientations change.

Anisotropy

The single-crystal arrows compare an edge with a body diagonal. In the polycrystal schematics, teal line motifs represent crystal orientations inside the grains.

Drag or use arrow keys to rotate. Top and Front are orthographic views. An end-on arrow uses a circled dot toward you or a circled cross away.

The main idea

  • A single crystal is usually anisotropic: a property can depend on the direction in which it is measured.
  • A randomly oriented polycrystal is approximately isotropic in bulk: the directional differences average out over many grains.
  • A textured polycrystal can be anisotropic: preferred crystal orientations can give the bulk material a directional response.

Random-orientation averaging describes a sufficiently large, representative set of grains. Each individual grain remains a crystal.
